## What it is in one paragraph

Aikido accepts a narrow point so the fight loses momentum, then redirects to action. It is not groveling. It is not agreement with the whole accusation. It is a clean pivot from "who is awful" to "what happens next."

## When it works / when it backfires

Use it when there is a real point you can concede without surrendering the decision.

Do not use it when the other person will treat any concession as a total confession. If the room is unsafe or the stakes are formal, keep the wording precise.

## Copy-paste scripts

<ScriptBlock label="Handled better">
  <p>"You are right, I could have handled that better. What would help now?"</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Next step">
  <p>"Fair point. What is the next step we should focus on?"</p>

<ScriptBlock label="Move forward">
  <p>"Noted. Let's talk about how to move forward."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Outcome">
  <p>"You may be right. What outcome are you looking for?"</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Repair frame">
  <p>"I hear that. What would make this better?"</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="No surrender">
  <p>"I can own that part. I am not taking on the whole claim."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

## Common mistakes

- Conceding the demand instead of the point.
- Saying it with sarcasm.
- Using it to skip a needed apology.
- Opening a huge new discussion with "what would help?"
- Forgetting to name your limit after the redirect.

Give one inch of truth, not the keys.

## Patterns this counters

Aikido can help with DARVO, moving goalposts, and public shaming because it can cool the attack while returning to the usable question.

<ScriptBlock label="Moving goalposts version">
  <p>"Fair point that the next version could be stronger. The original scope is complete."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

The point is not to win the clash. The point is to leave with the frame intact.
