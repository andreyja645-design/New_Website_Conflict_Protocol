## What it is in one paragraph

Labeling names the emotion in the room: frustration, hurt, fear, disappointment. Done well, it helps the other person feel heard while keeping the decision separate from the feeling.

## When it works / when it backfires

Use it when emotion is driving the conversation and a little recognition might lower the heat.

Do not use it like a diagnosis. If you label the wrong feeling with too much confidence, the conversation may become a correction festival.

## Copy-paste scripts

<ScriptBlock label="Frustrated">
  <p>"It seems like you are frustrated."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Hurt">
  <p>"You sound hurt by this."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Ignored">
  <p>"I get the sense you feel ignored."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Caught off guard">
  <p>"It looks like this caught you off guard."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Angry">
  <p>"I can hear that you are angry."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Add line">
  <p>"I can hear the disappointment, and my answer is still no."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

## Common mistakes

- Saying the label like a verdict.
- Naming motives instead of emotions.
- Using it to avoid your own answer.
- Over-labeling every sentence.
- Treating emotion as proof that you must comply.

Label softly. Then return to the decision.

## Patterns this counters

Labeling helps with silent treatment, empathy traps, and guilt trips because those patterns often make emotion the whole argument.

<ScriptBlock label="Silent treatment version">
  <p>"It seems like you are angry. I am available when you want to talk directly."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

The point is not to manage their feelings. The point is to stop being managed by them.
