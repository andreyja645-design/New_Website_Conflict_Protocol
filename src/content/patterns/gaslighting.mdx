## What it looks like

You bring up something that happened. They do not answer the thing. They put your memory on trial.

<ScriptBlock label="They say">
  <p>"That never happened. You always twist things."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="They say">
  <p>"I never said that. You heard what you wanted to hear."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="They say">
  <p>"You are too sensitive. Everyone else understood it was a joke."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

Now you are not talking about the joke, the promise, the deadline, or the lie. You are proving you are not dramatic, confused, unfair, or inventing things for sport. Cute little courtroom. Terrible use of your evening.

## Why it works on you

Gaslighting hooks the freeze and fawn reflex because it attacks your right to be sure. If you are used to staying safe by being reasonable, you may start over-proving. Dates. Receipts. Exact words. The shirt they were wearing. The air pressure in the room.

The trap is not one disagreement about memory. Normal people remember things differently and still stay curious. The pattern is using doubt as a weapon: "If you cannot prove every detail, I do not have to deal with the impact."

## The tell

Watch for the topic shift. You start with a concrete issue. They move to your character, your sanity, your tone, or whether you are allowed to trust yourself.

<ScriptBlock label="Anchor line">
  <p>"We remember it differently. I am not debating the memory all night."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="When they attack your character">
  <p>"I am not arguing about my character. I am talking about this specific issue."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="When they demand proof">
  <p>"I am not building a case file to be allowed to talk about this."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

The tell is the endless appeal process. Every answer creates a new objection. Every detail becomes a handle. You do not need a perfect transcript to set a limit.

## The trap

The trap is detail-chasing.

<ScriptBlock label="The line that feeds it">
  <p>"No, it did happen. It was Tuesday, in the kitchen, after I asked about the appointment, and I remember because..."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

That reply is understandable. It is also a buffet. They can challenge Tuesday, the kitchen, the appointment, your tone, and whether you "always do this." The original issue quietly leaves through the side door.

Try this instead:

<ScriptBlock label="Better">
  <p>"You do not have to agree with my memory for me to decide what I will do next."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Exit line">
  <p>"This is now about proving my reality. I am going to pause here."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

## Counters

Grey rock helps when the denial is bait. Keep the surface small. Do not give the conversation more loose parts to weaponize.

<ScriptBlock label="Low-detail version">
  <p>"I remember it differently. I am not discussing this further tonight."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Work version">
  <p>"Going forward, I need decisions confirmed in writing."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

<ScriptBlock label="Smallest usable version">
  <p>"We remember it differently."</p>
</ScriptBlock>

The win is not getting a confession. The win is leaving the memory trial before it eats the whole conversation.
